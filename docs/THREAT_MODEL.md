# Sealed threat model

Status: 2026-10-29. Written from the code after merge C, the release-candidate round (the hardening branches merged, composer 1.5.0) and the public fixes of the pre-publish review, the decisions through D-073, and the build, review and Docker check notes. The runtime has been tested locally, end to end on a local Tempo devnet with the dstack simulator (`make devnet-e2e`: 16 steps, 818 assertions, passing at the end of merge C), on a real Docker engine (the per-app network layout and sealed-egress), and against real dstack 0.6.1 quotes from three Google Cloud C3 TDX VMs running the upstream image, which `verifier/attest`, the quote verifier the key broker and `sealed verify` share, verifies offline against recorded Intel collateral (runtime spike, D-052; `TestRealGCPGolden`, `TestRealGCPMachineTypes`). Statements that depend on a boot of the production image are marked (first boot). At block 46329286 no platform image and no app is registered on mainnet yet, so the production measurements below are not recorded yet.

## What Sealed protects

Your data stays in your cloud account. Apps run in confidential VMs, we have no access path, and outbound traffic only reaches destinations you approve. Your wallet approves exactly which builds can unlock your data.

Concretely:

1. Secret release (P1). Customer secrets (app secrets, the data-disk key, the enclave signer seed, the gate password hash) are released only to a VM that proves with an Intel TDX quote that it runs an approved non-debug image and exactly the stack the customer's wallet approved onchain, built only from active releases. The customer's own key broker makes this decision in the customer's own project, and reads the approval at a chain head that is at most 60 seconds old, both when it reads the approval and when it seals the secrets (D-060); the platform is not in that path.
2. No operator access path (P2, P4). Production VMs have no SSH daemon, no getty or login (so no serial console login) and no service account, and are created with the serial port, OS Login and project SSH keys off. The platform's deployer identity in the customer project can create, start, stop and delete `sealed-*` VMs and disks, create, replace and delete `sealed-*` disk images, and read, write and delete objects in the `<project>-sealed-staging` bucket it imports images from (D-074), but cannot read Secret Manager, use the KMS key, change the key broker, attach a service account or open a shell. It can change the metadata of those VMs, so it can turn the serial port back on or add SSH keys; on this image neither reaches a login. A boot image it replaces measures differently, so the key broker refuses a VM booted from it. Its static IP permissions cover every address in the project, because Google Cloud cannot limit them by name, so Sealed needs a project of its own (see "Residual risks").
3. Egress control (P3). App containers have no route to the internet and cannot reach the VM's own listeners (D-064). The only proxy and DNS server, sealed-egress, enforces per-caller allowlists that are part of the measured stack, so every destination an app can reach is something the customer approved by approving the stackHash.
4. Content-free reporting (P5). What leaves the VM toward the platform is health, counts (egress counts use only allowlist entries and a fixed vocabulary, and are rounded down to two significant figures, D-065), signed usage totals and attestation evidence. Prompts and outputs never leave llm-gateway; app data never leaves the VM except to approved destinations.
5. Onchain privacy (P6). Deployment records hold hashes and addresses only: no customer config, IPs, hostnames or domains.
6. Independent verification (P7). Anyone with an evidence bundle and a Tempo RPC can re-check a deployment with `sealed verify`, without trusting the platform ([VERIFY.md](VERIFY.md)).
7. Data at rest. App data is on a LUKS2 disk whose key only an attested VM running a stack the customer approved can obtain, so a stopped VM's disk, a snapshot or a copy is unreadable to the operator and to anyone with project access. The key belongs to the customer and the config's salt (D-058), so it survives stack upgrades.

## What Sealed does not protect

Confidential VMs do not stop physical attacks on the server, bugs inside apps, or a cloud provider with physical access. In addition:

- A malicious or buggy app that the customer approved can misuse the data it is given, within its egress allowlist. Review of the catalog lowers this risk; it does not remove it.
- Destinations the customer adds to `egressAdditions` are trusted with whatever apps send them. Routes marked "data leaves your cloud" (external LLM providers) do exactly that.
- Prompts sent to `relay/<model>` leave the customer's cloud: they go to the platform relay, which runs in its own attested VM, and on to an attested inference provider. Provider attestation covers the provider's serving stack, not what a provider does outside it.
- Signals hidden in counts. An approved app controls how often it is blocked, what its health check answers and, for metered apps, its usage quantities. These are content-free, but they can carry a few bits per minute to the platform (accepted residual, D-065, see "Residual risks").
- TDX side channels, CPU microcode and TDX module bugs are covered only by Intel's TCB status reporting: the key broker and verifier reject quotes whose TCB status the policy does not allow (`UpToDate`, `SWHardeningNeeded` by default).
- Availability. The operator, Google or the customer can stop or delete VMs, block the network or withhold the Tempo RPC. Data on the persistent disk stays encrypted. Billing fails closed for failures on the customer's side, so blocking the chain, revoking the access key or exhausting its limit ends in the payment-needed page after the grace period, not in free usage. A platform outage is the exception (D-073): while the platform api cannot be reached, settlement is deferred and the grace period does not run (see "Residual risks").
- Integrity of the data disk against rollback. aes-xts has no integrity protection, so whoever controls the disk can corrupt blocks or restore an older copy of the whole disk. They cannot read or forge plaintext. A rollback returns app data to the older state; it does not make the agent bill a period it already signed (D-062, with the limit noted under "Residual risks").
- Supply chain before the catalog. An upstream project that ships malicious code in a pinned version is caught only by review, SBOMs and scans, and can be stopped by revoking the release onchain. That is mitigation, not prevention.
- What the customer approves. A wallet approval covers exactly the stackHash it signs. A customer who approves in the console trusts the console to show the stack it composed; `sealed deploy --plan` composes the same stack locally from the public releases and prints its stackHash, so the approval can be checked before it is signed.
- Weak gate passwords. The gate is bcrypt with rate limits; the password's strength is the customer's choice (`sealed secrets set gate --generate` makes a strong one).

## Trust anchors

| anchor | trusted for | if it fails | rotate or revoke |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intel TDX hardware, TDX module and Intel PCS collateral | quotes chain to Intel's root CA; TCB status | forged or stale quotes would pass | Intel TCB recovery; `tcb_policy` in `config/defaults.yaml` and the key broker's `SEALED_TCB_ALLOWED` |
| Google Cloud firmware (TDVF) | the code measured into MRTD, and the TD HOB and ACPI tables measured into RTMR0, which Google builds per machine type | firmware that misreports measurements | new image registration after a firmware change (below) |
| Google Cloud host settings | TD_ATTRIBUTES and XFAM, which the host chooses and no register measures | a TD launched with other attributes or CPU features | TD_ATTRIBUTES is checked by rule (no debug or migratable bit, `SEPT_VE_DISABLE` set, no reserved bit), and a quote that breaks it is refused; XFAM is pinned to an allowlist, `tdx.allowed_xfam` in `config/defaults.yaml` (today `0x602e7`, what C3 shows on every capture; D-077, amending D-070), and a quote with any other XFAM is refused |
| Google Cloud vTPM | sealing the dstack system disk seed (`key_provider: tpm`, D-017) | container layers on the system disk could be read by whoever controls the vTPM | not rotatable by us; app data does not depend on it |
| dstack OS image (MRTD, RTMR0 to RTMR2) | open-source build from Apache-2.0 sources without NVIDIA and ZFS (D-016), registered in PlatformRegistry with `isDebug` by the owner; RTMR1 and RTMR2 are computed from the image file, MRTD and RTMR0 are recorded per machine type; the customer's approval names the imageId, so a newly registered image runs only stacks a customer approved with it | a bad image could leak secrets after release | `revokeImage`; register a new image |
| Docker engine and kernel in the OS image | internal networks, Docker's internal-network drop rules on bridged traffic (`br_netfilter`), and the embedded DNS forwarding to sealed-egress, which needs Docker 26.1.3 or newer (dstack 0.6.1 ships 29.3.0) | a container could reach another network, or allowed names stop resolving (fails closed) | part of the measured image; a new image is registered |
| Release bundles | public source, digest-pinned images, SBOMs and scans, cosign keyless signature logged in Rekor, SLSA provenance, catalog review | approved apps could misbehave within their allowlist | `revokeRelease`, which blocks every stack that uses it at its next boot |
| Customer wallet | sole signer of `approveStack`, `revokeStack` and the access-key authorization | an attacker with the wallet can approve a malicious stack | move to a new wallet and re-bootstrap |
| Customer key broker and its Google Cloud project | runs from a digest-pinned public image with its own service account; customer address and RPC set by the customer | whoever controls the customer project can read the customer's own secrets | customer redeploys it |
| Tempo chain and the RPC endpoint | onchain state for approvals, images and releases | a lying RPC could claim approvals; a lagging one could hide a revocation, which the 60-second head check stops (D-060) | the customer chooses the RPC at bootstrap; the checks of one decision read at two pinned heads, each at most 60 seconds old: one for the image and release checks, a fresh one for the approval |
| OPS key and release CI | the only registrar of releases | a malicious release could be published (it still needs the customer's approval of a stack using it) | `revokeRegistrar`, `revokeRelease` |
| Oracle key (method 1 records) | attests that the platform verified evidence before recording it | misleading deployment records; never secret release | `removeSigner`; method 2 removes this anchor ([ZK_UPGRADE.md](ZK_UPGRADE.md)) |
| Platform | availability, the console, the relay, returning every usage report it stores for the deployment (D-062), and accepting new ones (fees are paid only for accepted reports) | outages, which defer fee settlement without starting the grace period (D-073); misleading records (caught by `sealed verify`); withheld usage reports (see "Residual risks") | none needed for secret safety |

## Measurement chain on Google Cloud

Runtime: dstack 0.6.1 (`Dstack-TEE/dstack`, Apache-2.0, D-015) on Google Cloud C3 Intel TDX. The production OS image is our own build of the dstack mkosi sources with the NVIDIA and ZFS components removed and `storage_fs: ext4` (D-016, `runtime/images/dstack-os`). The upstream image is used only for the runtime spike and is never registered as approved.

### Registers

| register | measures | on Google Cloud |
|---|---|---|
| MRTD | the TD's initial firmware | Google's TDVF build. Google is in the TCB for MRTD; it cannot be computed from our image. The same value was seen on three physical platforms and on two machine types. Trust on first use (below) |
| RTMR0 | firmware configuration, the TD HOB (memory map), ACPI tables and boot variables | built by Google's firmware, so it cannot be computed from our image. It depends on the machine type: on c3-standard-8 the TD HOB entry and the three ACPI entries differ from c3-standard-4, which gave one value on three physical platforms. Trust on first use, per machine type |
| RTMR1 | the boot path: the boot disk's GPT, the SHA-384 Authenticode of the unified kernel image (UKI) and of the `.linux` PE it loads, and the EFI boot actions | computed from the image file `disk.raw` |
| RTMR2 | systemd-stub's measurements of the UKI sections `.linux`, `.osrel`, `.cmdline`, `.initrd`, `.uname` and `.sbat` | computed from the image file. The command line carries `dstack.rootfs_hash`, the dm-verity root hash of the read-only root filesystem, so the whole OS is covered |
| RTMR3 | runtime events extended by dstack | replayed from the event log, never pinned (below) |

`measurementsHash = keccak256(mrtd || rtmr0 || rtmr1 || rtmr2)` (48 bytes each) is stored by `PlatformRegistry.addImage`, which also emits the raw registers. The key broker and the verifier require the quote's registers to hash to `getImage(imageId).measurementsHash` and `isApprovedImage(imageId)` to be true (Active and not debug).

Not in any register, and not in measurementsHash: TD_ATTRIBUTES and XFAM (chosen by the host; TD_ATTRIBUTES is checked by rule and XFAM pinned to an allowlist, below), MROWNER (set by Google, different on every instance), and MRCONFIGID and MROWNERCONFIG (zero on Google Cloud). dstack on Google Cloud does not consume the MR* fields.

The chain of trust is therefore MRTD (Google firmware), then RTMR1 (UKI Authenticode), then RTMR2 (command line with `dstack.rootfs_hash`, and the initrd), then the dm-verity root filesystem (dstack-util and the guest agent), then RTMR3 (compose hash, key provider and storage).

### Trust on first use

RTMR1 and RTMR2 are computed from the image, so only MRTD and RTMR0 are recorded trust-on-first-use (TOFU), per machine type (D-052):

1. CI builds the production image from pinned sources (`runtime/images/dstack-os`, reproducible) and publishes its identity: `os_image_hash`, the UKI Authenticode digests and the sha256 of the GCE tarball. RTMR1 and RTMR2 follow from `disk.raw` alone (`verifier/attest/testdata/gcp/tools/predict_rtmr12.py`).
2. `import-and-measure.sh` boots the image at least twice per machine type on C3 TDX, one VM at a time, with no service account, no external IP and no containers. Customer deployments run c3-standard-8 (D-037), so the production image is measured on c3-standard-8 before registration. Each quote must verify with Intel collateral, report_data must equal the fresh nonce, the TD must have production attributes, and RTMR0 to RTMR2 must replay from the event log.
3. The boots of a machine type must agree, and RTMR1 and RTMR2 must equal the values computed from the image. Machine types with identical measurements share one image entry; each other machine type gets its own (`addImage(..., isDebug = false, sourceURI)`, with the machine types in the version).
4. The values are recorded here, with the image's TD_ATTRIBUTES and XFAM.

Until Google's signed firmware endorsements are checked (planned, not implemented: Google publishes a signed endorsement per MRTD; the verifier pins MRTD through PlatformRegistry only), MRTD and RTMR0 are trusted because clean boots on Google's hardware produced them, not because they were derived from source. A Google firmware update changes MRTD or RTMR0 and needs a new registration. Deprecating or revoking the old image stops every deployment on it at once (contract review logic-F7), so new images are registered and stacks moved before an old image is retired.

### Quotes and quoteHash (D-052)

dstack's GetQuote on Google Cloud returns an 8000-byte buffer. The signed quote is 4935 bytes (a 48-byte header, the 584-byte TD body, a 4-byte signature length and 4299 bytes of signature data); the rest is zero padding, on every capture. Nothing signs the padding, so a verifier that accepted it would let one quote take many quoteHash values, and quoteHash is what the onchain record and heartbeat carry. So:

- Every producer trims to the signed bytes: Go `attest.TrimQuote`, and TypeScript `trimQuote` in `@sealed/core`, which sealed-agent uses for key release, evidence and `/attest`. Trimming cuts zero padding only, and refuses non-zero trailing bytes and truncated quotes.
- quoteHash is `sha256` of the signed bytes only.
- The key broker and the verifiers refuse a quote with any trailing bytes.
- They also refuse a PCK certificate chain that is not in canonical PEM form (Go fuzzing): only the decoded certificates are signed, and PEM decoding skips spaces, tabs and CRs in the base64 and ignores its padding bits, so the chain's spelling was as unsigned as the padding.

The simulator's quotes have no padding, which is why only real hardware showed this. One malleability stays: each of the quote's two ECDSA signatures also verifies as (r, n-s), and real quote generation uses either form, so one attestation has up to four quoteHash values (`TestQuoteECDSATwinsVerify`). Nothing relies on quoteHash being unique: freshness comes from report_data (the release nonce, or nonce2 and attestedAt since D-047), and a heartbeat needs a later attestedAt.

### RTMR3 and the compose hash

dstack extends RTMR3 at boot with a fixed event sequence: `system-preparing`, `app-id`, `compose-hash`, `gpu-policy-hash`, `instance-id`, `boot-mr-done`, `key-provider` (`{"name":"tpm","id":""}`), `storage-fs` (`ext4`), `storage-encrypted` (`1`), `system-ready`. Each digest is `sha384(le32(0x08000001) || ":" || name || ":" || payload)` folded from 48 zero bytes. The runtime spike confirmed this exact sequence on real hardware.

- The `compose-hash` payload is `sha256(app-compose.json)`, which is the stackHash. The composed stack includes every image digest, every fragment, the egress allowlists, the Caddyfile, the host firewall script and the public config, so one changed byte anywhere changes the stackHash.
- The key broker and the verifier require exactly this sequence, reject unknown events, replay it to the quote's RTMR3 and require the compose hash to equal `sha256(appCompose)`. RTMR3 differs per VM (`instance-id` mixes in the vTPM key), so it is replayed, never pinned.
- Apps cannot extend RTMR3 (dstack 0.6 removed `EmitEvent`), and only sealed-agent can reach the dstack socket.
- Unmeasured host inputs on the dstack shared disk (`.sys-config.json`, `.instance_info`, `.user-config`) never drive a security decision. A registry mirror set there can only cause a denial of service, because every image is pulled by digest.

### TD attributes and TCB

- TD_ATTRIBUTES: no debug bits, not migratable, `SEPT_VE_DISABLE` set, no reserved bits. On Google Cloud C3, TD_ATTRIBUTES is `0x10000000` (`SEPT_VE_DISABLE` only) and XFAM is `0x602e7` (x87, SSE, AVX, AVX-512, PKRU and AMX). The host chooses both and no register measures them. The key broker and the verifier refuse a TD_ATTRIBUTES value that breaks the rule above (so `0x10000000` passes and a debug or migratable TD does not), and refuse an XFAM that is not in `tdx.allowed_xfam` in `config/defaults.yaml` (D-077, amending D-070), so a host that launches the TD with other CPU features gets nothing. The list holds the values measured on the registered machine shapes, today only `0x602e7` (every capture on c3-standard-4 and c3-standard-8); it is one list for every image, not a value per image. Both values are recorded in the broker's decision log and the verifier's `td_attributes` data.
- Debug images are registered with `isDebug = true` and never receive anything: the broker has no debug secret set. Google Cloud does not launch debug TDs.
- TCB: the quote, the PCK chain, the QE report, collateral signatures and expiry, and the CRLs are checked; the platform, TDX module and QE TCB levels are combined as Intel's QVL does and must be in `tcb_policy.allowed_statuses`. Every real capture so far was `UpToDate` for the platform, the TDX module and the QE, with no advisories.

### report_data bindings (D-010, D-047)

Every construction is `sha256(tag || fields)` followed by 32 zero bytes, so a quote made for one purpose is useless for another:

| use | fields |
|---|---|
| key release, `sealed/kb-release/v1` | broker nonce (single use, 5 minutes), ephemeral X25519 public key |
| evidence, `sealed/evidence/v1` | stackHash, configHash, enclaveSigner, nonce2, `uint64be(attestedAt)` |
| live attest, `sealed/attest/v1` | caller's nonce, `sha256` of the served TLS certificate's public key, enclaveSigner |

### Disks (D-017, D-058)

- dstack system disk: encrypted by dstack with a seed sealed in the Google vTPM under PCRs 0, 2 and 14 (PCR14 carries the same runtime events as RTMR3). It holds container images and container layers. Its protection trusts Google's vTPM. The runtime spike confirmed that the seed unseals after a stop and start of the same VM (D-052). App data should not be written there: every app volume is a bind of the encrypted data disk.
- `sealed-data` disk: LUKS2, aes-xts-plain64, 512-bit, with the volume key and keyslot passphrase derived by HKDF from the broker's `diskKey`. Before mounting, the agent requires exactly one expected keyslot, one crypt segment, no tokens and no reencryption requirement, and after unlocking it checks the device-mapper table for exactly the derived key. A header the host prepared (cipher_null, a keyslot any passphrase opens, a volume key the host knows) is never mounted.
- `diskKey = HMAC_KMS("sealed/disk/v2" || customer || salt)` and `signerSeed = HMAC_KMS("sealed/signer/v2" || customer || salt)`, computed by the customer's Cloud KMS HMAC key (D-058, replacing the deploymentId of D-011). The root key never leaves KMS. A reboot or a stack upgrade (new releases, same salt) keeps the data disk and the enclave signer; the broker still releases them only to an approved stack of that customer. A customer who wants a fresh disk uses a new salt.
- A Google Cloud stop is not a clean shutdown: the spike's next boot recovered the filesystem journal. The data volume is ext4 with journaling on the LUKS volume, so it recovers the same way.

## The key release decision

The key broker limits requests per client address (2 releases per second with a burst of 20, four times that for nonces), bounds the request body (2 MiB, read with a 10 s deadline) before it allocates anything, takes a release slot only after the body has been read and validated, and writes requests that fail early to the decision log only in truncated form and asynchronously (D-066). It answers `POST /v1/release` only after these checks pass, in this order, each logged with its reason and hashes to the customer's Cloud Logging. An allowed release is answered only after its log entry is written.

1. The nonce was issued by this broker, is unexpired and unused. It is consumed before any other check.
2. The quote verifies with Intel collateral, its TCB status is allowed, and it is exactly the signed quote with no trailing bytes (D-052). No quote, a non-TDX blob or a simulated quote is refused by production builds (the production binary has no development flags at all).
3. report_data equals the key-release construction for this nonce and ephemeral key.
4. The RPC serves chain 4217 (or the configured chain on local runs), and the block the image, release and recomputation reads are pinned to has a timestamp within 60 seconds of the broker's clock (configurable, at most 300). Otherwise the broker denies with `chain_stale`, so a stale or lagging RPC cannot hide a revocation (D-060).
5. The image exists, its measurements match, MRCONFIGID and MROWNERCONFIG are zero, XFAM is in `tdx.allowed_xfam` (D-077), it is not a debug image, TD_ATTRIBUTES has no debug, migratable or reserved bit and has `SEPT_VE_DISABLE`, and the image is approved.
6. RTMR3 replays with the exact event sequence and its compose hash equals `sha256(appCompose)`.
7. `config.customer` is the bootstrap customer, and the config names this broker's chain, registries, FeeSplitter and fee token. These cheap checks run before the recomputation.
8. The composer recomputes `appCompose` byte for byte from the config and the onchain releases' bundles, each fetched by the D-046 asset rule and accepted only when its sha256 equals the onchain bundleHash. Every `same_as` reference must point at a secret whose own manifest entry lists the referencing app in `share_with` (D-059).
9. The broker pins a fresh head with the test of step 4, because step 8 can take a while. At that block `isStackApproved(customer, stackHash)` holds, and the approval's releaseIds and imageId equal the config's (D-014).
10. Only then, and only if that head is still at most 60 seconds old (otherwise `chain_stale`): the stack's declared secrets, `diskKey` and `signerSeed`, HPKE-sealed (X25519, HKDF-SHA256, AES-256-GCM) to the ephemeral key with the nonce as associated data.

## Residual risks

From the contract review (`contracts/README.md`; no critical or high findings, every medium fixed):

- Fees keep flowing for a deployment whose stack, release or image was revoked, because past usage must stay payable (logic-F3, funds-F-07). Levers: `setMaintainerShare(app, 0)`, `setMaintainer`, `closeDeployment`.
- Daily caps use fixed UTC days, so up to twice a daily cap can be paid around midnight (funds-F-04).
- Closed deployments cannot pay; the stop flow settles the last period before `closeDeployment` (funds-F-05).
- TIP-20 issuer policies (TIP-403) can block a recipient or FeeSplitter itself (funds-F-08).
- deploymentId is not bound onchain to `(customer, stackHash, salt)`; the oracle recomputes it before signing (crypto-SEAL-04).
- Method 1 trusts a single oracle signer, and events do not name the signer; the indexer recovers it from transaction input (crypto-SEAL-07). Method 2 needs a report_data v2 first (crypto-SEAL-03, [ZK_UPGRADE.md](ZK_UPGRADE.md)).
- A signed record stays registrable by anyone for `maxAttestationAge` (1 day) unless ops lowers it (logic-F8, partly fixed by CR-3).
- Deprecating an image stops every deployment on it at once (logic-F7).

From the runtime work, the runtime spike, the Docker checks, and the reviews of the key broker, sealed-egress, sealed-agent, the composer and the verifier:

- Google firmware and the vTPM are in the TCB. MRTD and RTMR0 are TOFU per machine type until Google's firmware endorsements are checked; RTMR1 and RTMR2 are computed from the image.
- MROWNER (per instance), MRCONFIGID and MROWNERCONFIG are not in measurementsHash; dstack on Google Cloud does not consume them. TDX 1.5 relaunch-advised statuses are reported as OutOfDate, which the default policy rejects.
- If current C3 hosts report OutOfDate, a customer who allows it (`SEALED_TCB_ALLOWED`) accepts known TCB issues; that is the customer's security decision.
- An Intel PCS outage stops new releases once the broker's collateral cache expires: the broker answers 503 `unavailable` (the quote was not judged), and the agent retries. Simulated quotes and quotes that fail for any other reason are still `quote_invalid`.
- The head check (D-060) catches a stale or lagging RPC node. An RPC that lies can also forge a fresh timestamp, so the RPC the customer chooses at bootstrap stays a trust anchor.
- The broker keeps nonces in a bounded store in memory on one Cloud Run instance (100,000 live nonces, oldest evicted first; a journal is an option but needs durable storage, which the bootstrap does not mount, D-066). Per-client limits apply to `GET /v1/nonce` (four times the release rate), keyed by the last `X-Forwarded-For` hop, so a client with many addresses can still evict nonces by issuing more than 100,000 inside the seconds a booting agent holds its own.
- The release is not bound to the customer's infrastructure: any TDX VM that runs the approved image and the approved stack gets the same release, wherever it runs. Secrets stay inside attested VMs running the approved code, and per-deployment fee caps key on the deploymentId, so copies share them.
- The agent authenticates the broker's answer only through TLS to the broker URL in the config. Whoever could impersonate that URL gets nothing released, but could hand a fresh deployment a disk key and secrets it knows.
- Every stack of the customer approved with the same salt gets the same disk key and enclave signer (D-058). After an upgrade, the old stack can still open the data disk while its approval stands. A customer who wants it locked out revokes it (`revokeStack`); a customer who wants a fresh disk uses a new salt. The salt is part of the config, so the customer's approval covers it. An upgrade still changes the deploymentId, so the upgraded stack gets its own onchain record.
- The broker and verifiers recompute only stacks of their own composer version; each version bump needs a broker image built with it before stacks of that version can boot.
- The key broker does not check release signatures. Release CI signs each release with cosign keyless (logged in Rekor) and adds SLSA provenance, and `sealed verify` and sealed-verifierd check both for every release (`release_signature`, [VERIFY.md](VERIFY.md#what-each-check-means)). The key broker, which decides secret release, accepts a bundle whose sha256 equals its onchain bundleHash and checks neither, so for secret release a release is anchored by the OPS key and the release CI that registers it ("Trust anchors"). A dev verification (`Options.AcceptUnsignedReleases`, set by `--dev-allow-simulated` under `SEALED_DEV_SIM=1`) passes unsigned releases with a `DEV:` detail.
- `same_as` gives one app another app's secret when the source secret lists that app in `share_with` (D-059), as llm-gateway's `APP_KEY_CODING_AGENT` does for coding-agent. Catalog review checks every `share_with` entry.
- sealed-egress identifies callers by source address. Dropping `NET_RAW` is load-bearing: on a real Docker engine, a container with Docker's default capabilities forged a DNS query from the agent's address on its app network and egress counted it for the agent. Without `NET_RAW` every forging path failed. Docker's internal-network rules confine a forged source to the container's own app network, and they apply to bridged traffic only because Docker loads `br_netfilter` (`bridge-nf-call-iptables=1`), which the A8 run checks in the CVM (first boot). Proxy credentials (`proxyAuth`) exist but are off.
- Name resolution for apps goes through Docker's embedded DNS, which forwards to sealed-egress from internal-only networks only on Docker 26.1.3 or newer. From 26.0.0 to 26.1.2 it forwards nothing, so allowed names would not resolve (fail closed). dstack 0.6.1 ships Docker 29.3.0, and any change of the OS image must keep Docker at 26.1.3 or newer. The A8 run asserts that a random subdomain from an app is counted as `unlisted:53` for that app's caller (first boot).
- On a reboot, dockerd restarts containers before dstack runs `pre_launch_script`, so Caddy and sealed-egress run for a moment without the host rules. Apps are on internal networks and cannot use that window. Whether a failing `pre_launch_script` stops the boot is checked at the first boot.
- Covert channel through counts (accepted residual, D-065). Egress counts, app health and usage quantities are content-free, but an app that controls them can still carry information to the platform. P5 allows this. The agent review estimated about 100 bits per minute before rounding: an app chooses how often it is blocked per key (up to 50 keys per side in a report every minute, each with up to 100 proxy requests and 100 DNS queries a second per caller), what its health check answers, and the payer counters it drives. Counts sent to the platform are now rounded down to two significant figures (sealed-egress `GET /v1/stats` and the agent's health report), so a change below about one percent of a count is invisible; that narrows the channel but does not close it, and its rounded bandwidth has not been measured. Usage quantities stay exact, because billing needs them, and the fees they produce are public onchain. Coarser options (log2 buckets, longer windows, per-key detail only for the customer) remain open.
- Disk rollback and billing (D-062). The agent finds the periods it already signed in the usage reports the platform stores (`GET /v1/deployments/:id/usage-reports`), keeping only reports that verify against its own enclave signer, and in onchain `usagePaid`. The rebuild trusts the platform to return every report it stores: the platform cannot forge a report, but it can withhold stored ones. Whoever controls both the disk and the platform api could restore an older disk, withhold the reports signed after it, and get an open period billed twice, bounded by the access-key limit and the FeeSplitter caps. The withheld report's usageHash stays visible onchain in `FeesPaid` with no stored report to match it, so the double billing is detectable afterwards; the agent does not check `FeesPaid` against its rebuilt ledger yet.
- Usage lost on a disk rollback (D-089 B3, accepted residual). Open-period usage accepted after the last signed report lives only in the disk state. A rollback discards it, and the boot rebuild (D-062) restores the ledger from signed reports and onchain `usagePaid` only, so that usage is never reported and never billed. This is operator-side revenue loss, not double billing: nothing bills usage that no report covers. The billing soak saw 6,256 tokens and 3 pages lost on one rollback and 44,598 tokens and 20 pages on another (docs/notes/billing-soak.md). A crash (not a rollback) in the batched-save window no longer loses acknowledged usage: the agent write-ahead-journals each accepted event before it answers 202 and replays the journal at boot (D-089 B2).
- Pricing remainder lost on a disk rollback (D-089 B4, accepted residual). The per-(app, unit) pricing remainder, the sub-base-unit carry held between reports, is rolled back with the disk and is not reconciled from onchain `usagePaid`, so a rollback can mis-bill a series by at most one base unit (1e-6 USD). Negligible, and operator-side.
- Platform outages defer fees (D-073). The agent pays only reports the platform accepted (D-062), so while the platform api is unreachable, or its stored reports cannot be read in this boot, settlement is deferred: those fees are not paid and the grace period does not run, and a grace period already running for a customer-side failure is paused. The customer controls the VPC firewall, so a customer who blocks the platform api delays every fee for as long as the block lasts and keeps using the apps; the deferred fees are paid once the platform is reachable again. The operator bears that delay, not the customer. A deployment the oracle has not registered yet (`deployment_not_registered`) is a platform-side wait as well, because only the oracle and the ops key register it from the agent's evidence: its backlog is paid once it is registered. Two residuals follow, both accepted by D-073 (the operator bears them): a customer who blocks the platform api at boot runs unbilled until it unblocks, and a customer who revokes the stack approval before the oracle registers the deployment runs unbilled until the next boot, which the broker then refuses. Counting `deployment_not_registered` once the api answered the evidence with 422 `evidence_rejected` naming `stack_approved` would close the second window (lead decision). A chain read error (`rpc_error`) stays on the customer's side, because the customer chooses the RPC and controls the VPC firewall; an outage of the public Tempo RPC therefore counts toward the grace period too.
- attestedAt in evidence comes from the guest clock, which the host sets. `secure_time` stays off (D-073), because the bootstrap firewall allows egress on 443 and 53 only, so NTS cannot work. attestedAt is bound in report_data (D-047) and checked against the oracle's clock (15-minute window, at most 5 minutes ahead), the block time and `maxAttestationAge`, which bounds what a lying host clock can do; `sealed verify --live` with your own nonce does not depend on it.
- XFAM is pinned to an allowlist, not per image (D-077): the key broker and the verifier accept any value in `tdx.allowed_xfam` (today only `0x602e7`) for every registered image. A machine family with other CPU features (C4, for example) needs a new entry, which ships in new key broker, verifierd and CLI builds. TD_ATTRIBUTES is checked by rule.
- Debug VMs are the one exception to "no service account": a VM booted from an `isDebug` image may carry the bootstrap's `sealed-debug-logs` account with only the `logging.write` scope. Debug images never get production secrets, a production disk key or a production data disk, because PlatformRegistry never approves them and `sealed deploy` refuses to attach a production deployment's disk.
- Boot progress appears on the VM's serial output, which project members with the right permission can read; nothing secret is printed there and container output never reaches it.
- The deployer can change the metadata of `sealed-*` VMs (`compute.instances.setMetadata`), so it can turn `serial-port-enable` back on or add `ssh-keys` at any time. The production image has no sshd, getty or login (`runtime/images/dstack-os/check_image.py` fails the image build if one is present), so neither gives a shell. The kernel does not answer SysRq keys on the serial console either: the image's kernel is built with `CONFIG_MAGIC_SYSRQ=n`, `usr/lib/sysctl.d/90-sealed-sysrq.conf` sets `kernel.sysrq = 0`, and `check_image.py` fails the build if SysRq could be on (D-082). The residual is accepted (D-082): run Sealed in a dedicated project, and once that project is in an organization, set the `compute.disableSerialPortAccess` policy.
- The deployer's static IP permissions (`compute.addresses.create`, `delete`, `get` and `use`) are granted on the whole project, because IAM conditions cannot name addresses. In a project that holds other workloads, the deployer could delete their static addresses or attach a free one to a Sealed VM. Run Sealed in a project of its own.

From payments (relay and payer, after the fixes of D-054 and D-067):

- The relay sees prompts sent to `relay/*` inside its attested VM.
- Per-request relay charges are final. The `relay/*` routes in llm-gateway set `max_retries: 0` (D-067), so the gateway does not pay a failed request again, but a client that retries a failed request pays again.
- The access key's escrow calls (`open`, `topUp`, `requestClose`, `withdraw`) cannot carry a payee rule onchain, because the keychain refuses recipient rules on those selectors (`InvalidCallScope`, D-054a). The channel payee is enforced only by sealed-mpp-payer's payment firewall, which accepts only the treasury the relay attestation proved as payee, only the TIP-1034 escrow precompile and only FEE_TOKEN. A compromised payer, or any code holding the enclave key, could open a channel to another payee within the key's limit; escrow deposits do count against that limit. `approve` (to FeeSplitter) and `transferWithMemo` (to the treasury) are limited onchain.
- The payer holds the enclave key, which also signs usage reports, health reports and fee payments. A separate key for the payer is a follow-up.
- A passing relay verdict stays in force for up to 15 minutes, so a revocation of the relay's stack or releases is noticed up to 15 minutes late.
- Tinfoil is accepted for any Sigstore-signed release of its router, without a review of each release. NEAR AI attests its gateway, not the model nodes behind it, and its model and GPU evidence are not checked.
- The default daily spend limit (2 USD) is below the app-hour fees of a full stack (3.60 USD a day). The console warns when the selected apps' fees exceed the limit (D-067). A customer who keeps the lower limit sees settlement fail and, after the grace period, the payment-needed page: billing fails closed.
